## Hints

- Is every ugly piece of code technical debt? What makes a shortcut "debt" rather than just a shortcut?
- How would you decide which of twenty known problems to fix first?
- A product manager says there is no time for refactoring this quarter. What do you say?

## Answer

**Technical debt** is the future cost of a design or implementation decision that was faster now: the "interest" is the extra time every later change costs, the "principal" is the work to fix it. Ward Cunningham's metaphor was about **deliberate** shortcuts taken to learn faster and repaid soon. Martin Fowler's quadrant adds two axes: deliberate vs inadvertent, prudent vs reckless. "We'll ship without tests to hit the launch and add them next sprint" is prudent and deliberate; "nobody knew what layering was" is reckless and inadvertent; "now we know how it should have been done" is prudent and inadvertent, and normal.

Not everything ugly is debt. Code that is never touched costs no interest. Debt is only worth repaying where it is **slowing you down or risking an incident**.

**Kinds you should be able to name:** code (duplication, god components, no types), architecture (wrong boundaries, shared mutable state, a monolith that should have been split or the reverse, #138), test debt (no tests around the scary part, flaky suites, #137), dependency and platform debt (unsupported React or Node versions, abandoned packages, known CVEs, #375), infrastructure (manual deploys, no rollback, #406), documentation and knowledge (one person understands it).

**How to find it:** hotspot analysis (files with high **churn × complexity** from git history), incident postmortems (#440), lead time and change-failure rate (#404), onboarding friction ("what took the new hire three days?"), and the places where estimates are always wrong.

**How to prioritise:** by interest paid, not by how much it offends you. A rough score: how often is this area touched × how much extra each change costs × risk of outage or security issue. A messy module nobody will change this year scores zero. Keep a visible **debt register** (tickets with the symptom, the cost, the proposed fix and a size), reviewed in planning like any other backlog.

**How to pay it down:**

- **Boy-scout rule:** leave touched code a bit better, inside the feature's PR, with the refactor in its own commit (#416) so review stays easy.
- **Tie repayment to features:** "to build X safely we first need to extract Y", estimated into the feature. This is how most debt actually gets paid.
- **A standing allocation** (10-20% of capacity) for debt tickets. Dedicated "refactoring sprints" rarely survive the first urgent request; small, continuous payments do.
- **Characterisation tests first** when touching untested code, then refactor in small, reversible steps; the **strangler fig** pattern for whole subsystems (#425).
- **Stop creating new debt** in the same place: a linter rule, an ADR, an architectural boundary check in CI.

**Talking to product:** translate into their currency. "Every change to checkout takes two weeks instead of three days and we had two incidents there in Q2; two weeks of cleanup brings the next five features in a month earlier" is persuasive; "the code is ugly" is not. Offer options with costs, agree on what you are *not* fixing, and report the outcome afterwards so the next conversation is easier (#491).

**Answering as a story (STAR):** situation (a component everyone feared to touch), the measurement that proved it (lead time, bug count), what you proposed and how you scoped it, what you did (tests, incremental extraction behind a flag), the result (changes in days, zero incidents since), and what you would do differently.

**Follow-up:** when is it right to *take on* debt deliberately? (Validating a product bet, a deadline with real business consequences, or code you expect to throw away. Write down the decision and the repayment plan.)
