## Hints

- What do you need to know before writing a single line of code?
- How do you check that the problem is real, and how big it is?
- Which number would tell you two weeks after release that it worked, and which would tell you that you broke something else?

## Answer

"You get a problem, not a specification" means you own the whole loop: understand, decide, build, **measure**. Example problem: *"Too many patients drop out before their consultation."*

**1. Understand the problem and why it matters**

- Talk to the people who own it and who see it: the business owner, operations, customer support (what do patients complain about?), doctors.
- Look at the data: a funnel from search → slot chosen → payment → confirmed → joined the call. Where is the drop, since when, for whom?
- Turn it into a measurable statement with a baseline: "38% of patients who choose a slot don't complete payment, up from 30% in Q2; the cost is about N consultations a week."

**2. Find the cause before choosing the solution**

- Form hypotheses and check them with data: segment by device, app version, payment method, insurer, time of day, how far ahead the slot is.
- Example finding: the drop is concentrated on mobile web at the card payment step, after the 3-D Secure redirect. Error logs show the return URL loses the session for one bank. That's a bug, not a UX redesign.
- Don't stop at the first explanation that fits (#549).

**3. Choose a solution with the stakeholders**

- List options with cost, expected impact, risk and reversibility; prefer the smallest change that tests the hypothesis.
- Be ready to say "the data suggests something other than what you asked for" (#491). You're co-deciding **what and why**, not only how.

**4. Define success before building**

- **Primary metric:** payment completion on mobile web.
- **Guardrail metrics:** refunds, support tickets, booking errors, latency, doctor utilisation.
- **Target and window:** back to around 30% drop-off within two weeks.
- **How it's measured:** which events and dashboard. If the event tracking is missing, adding it is the first task.

**5. Ship incrementally and watch**

Put it behind a feature flag, roll it out to a percentage or a segment, and watch the metrics the same day. Use an A/B test if there's enough traffic; otherwise compare before and after carefully (seasonality, marketing campaigns, flu season).

**6. Close the loop**

Report the result honestly, including "it didn't move the metric". Then iterate, roll back or try the next hypothesis, and write down what you learned.

**Pitfalls:** measuring output ("the feature shipped") instead of outcome, vanity metrics, changing several things at once, too small a sample, the novelty effect, ignoring guardrail metrics, and averages that hide segments (Simpson's paradox).

**In an interview, answer with a real story** in this shape: the problem as it came to you, how you sized it, what you found, what you built, the numbers before and after, and what you'd do differently.

**Follow-up:** the metric didn't move after your change. What do you do? (Check that the change actually reached users and the tracking works, re-segment the data, revisit the hypothesis, and decide whether to iterate or remove the change. A feature that doesn't help is complexity to delete.)
