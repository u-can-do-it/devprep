## Hints

- How do you find out where a junior actually is, and what they want to learn?
- When do you give the answer, and when do you ask a question that leads them to it?
- How do you measure that your mentoring worked?

## Answer

For senior roles, interviewers want evidence that you **multiply the team**, not just ship your own work.

**What effective mentoring looks like:**
- **Understand the person:** their current skills, goals, and how they like to learn. Agree on a few concrete growth areas (e.g. testing, debugging, owning a feature end to end).
- **Calibrated autonomy:** give tasks slightly above their level, with a clear goal and constraints. Let them struggle productively, with a known way to ask for help (and an explicit "ask after 30–60 minutes stuck" rule).
- **Ask before telling:** "What have you tried? What do you think is happening?" builds problem-solving. Give direct answers when time matters or it's pure trivia.
- **Code reviews as teaching:** explain *why*, link to resources, separate blocking issues from nitpicks, praise good decisions.
- **Pairing:** let them drive while you navigate.
- **Psychological safety:** normalize questions and mistakes, and share your own.
- **Growing visibility:** let them present their work, own a small project, and get credit.

**Answer with a real example (STAR):** who you mentored, where they started, what you did specifically (regular 1:1s, a learning plan, pairing on their first on-call week), and the **outcome** (they now own a service, got promoted, onboard others). Use your own experience.

**What interviewers look for:** patience, structured support, adapting to the individual, feedback skills, and pride in others' growth.

**Red flags:**
- "I just tell them how to do it" or rewriting their code yourself.
- Mentoring seen as a distraction from "real work".
- Only examples of correcting people, never growing them.
- Taking credit for the mentee's achievements.
