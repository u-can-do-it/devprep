## Hints

- What would make a technical disagreement objective instead of a battle of opinions?
- When is it right to "disagree and commit"?
- How do you decide whether a decision is worth fighting for at all?

## Answer

> Senior engineers disagree often, and the interviewer wants to see you argue well and still help the team decide. Show that you first understand the other view, agree on criteria like cost, speed and reversibility, and settle it with evidence such as a spike, benchmark or written trade-offs. Spend effort in proportion to the stakes. If there's no consensus, the decision-maker decides and you *disagree and commit*. Back this up with a real STAR example, ideally one where you weren't right.

Interviewers want to see that you can advocate strongly for your views while staying collaborative, and that you help teams make good decisions — not just win arguments.

**A strong approach to describe:**

1. **Understand first:** ask why the other person prefers their option; restate their position to confirm you understand it. Often the disagreement is about different assumptions or priorities (speed vs maintainability).
2. **Agree on criteria:** performance, cost, time-to-market, team familiarity, operational burden, reversibility.
3. **Use evidence:** benchmarks, a small spike or prototype, production data, prior incidents, written tradeoff comparisons (an RFC or ADR).
4. **Match effort to stakes:** for reversible, low-impact choices, defer or let the owner decide quickly; save deep debate for one-way-door decisions (data models, core infrastructure).
5. **Decide and commit:** if consensus isn't reached, the agreed decision-maker decides. Then **disagree and commit** — support the decision fully, and record the risks so they can be revisited.
6. **Follow up:** define what signal would make you revisit the choice.

Back it with a concrete STAR example: the disagreement, how you structured the discussion, the outcome, and what happened later (including if you turned out to be wrong).

**What interviewers look for:** humility, data-driven reasoning, writing things down, psychological safety for others to speak up, and no lingering resentment.

**Red flags:**

- Always being right in your stories.
- Pulling rank or going around people.
- Passive-aggressive compliance or reopening settled decisions repeatedly.
- Treating every choice as a hill to die on.
