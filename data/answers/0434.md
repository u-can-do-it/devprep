## Hints

- Do you start by reading everything, or by building something small?
- How do you separate the 20% of a technology you need now from the rest?
- How do you check that you're writing idiomatic code and not just code that works?

## Answer

Tech changes constantly, so interviewers want evidence that you can become **productive quickly and responsibly** in unfamiliar territory.

**Describe a repeatable approach:**
1. **Start from the goal:** what do I need to deliver with it? That defines the minimum to learn.
2. **Official docs and the mental model first:** the "getting started" guide plus the core concepts (e.g. for Next.js App Router: Server vs Client Components, caching, Server Actions).
3. **Build a small spike** that touches the real use case. Learning by doing, with fast feedback.
4. **Read good existing code:** the codebase, the official examples, well-known open-source projects.
5. **Use AI assistants critically:** for explanations and boilerplate, while verifying against the docs, because they can be outdated or wrong.
6. **Get feedback early:** ask an experienced colleague to review the first PR for idiomatic usage.
7. **Consolidate:** write notes or a short internal guide, teach someone else.

**Then back it with a real example of your own (STAR):** the technology and why you had to learn it, the steps you took, how long until you shipped something useful, and what you would do differently. A template: *"We needed [tech] for [goal]. I knew [related tech], so I focused on [key concepts], built [spike], got [review/feedback], and shipped [result] within [timeframe]."*

**What interviewers look for:** a structured method, curiosity, pragmatism (learning *enough* to deliver), and humility in asking for help.

**Red flags:**
- "I just watch a course and then I know it." No practice, no feedback.
- Learning only through tutorials with nothing shipped.
- Blind copy-pasting from AI or Stack Overflow.
- Chasing every new trend without a reason.
