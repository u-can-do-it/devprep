## Hints

- Is the hard part throughput, or many patients competing for the same few slots?
- Payment or insurance verification takes a few minutes. Where is the slot during that time?
- A doctor works 9:00–17:00 Warsaw time. What happens to that schedule on the night the clocks change?

## Answer

**Requirements to clarify first**

- Patients book a specific doctor and time, or ask for the **first available** doctor of a specialty.
- Doctors define recurring working hours with exceptions (leave, extra shifts); consultations take 10–30 minutes.
- A slot is **held** while the patient pays or the insurer confirms coverage.
- Never double-book; cancellations and rescheduling; reminders; no-shows.
- Scale: thousands of consultations a day means a few bookings per second at peak, but around 100× more availability reads. **Contention on popular slots**, not raw throughput, is the hard part. Monday mornings and flu season are peaks.

**Data model**

```
doctors(id, specialty, timezone)
schedule_rules(doctor_id, weekday, start_local, end_local, slot_minutes, valid_from, valid_to)
schedule_exceptions(doctor_id, starts_at, ends_at, kind)
slots(id, doctor_id, starts_at, ends_at, status, held_by, hold_expires_at, version)   -- UTC, generated weeks ahead
appointments(id, slot_id, patient_id, status, idempotency_key UNIQUE, created_at)
```

**Materialised slots** (a job expands the rules into rows for the next few weeks) make both searching and locking simple, and work the same in MySQL and PostgreSQL. In PostgreSQL you can instead skip slot rows, compute availability on the fly, store `doctor_id`, `starts_at` and `ends_at` on the appointment and prevent overlaps with an exclusion constraint:

```sql
CREATE EXTENSION IF NOT EXISTS btree_gist;
ALTER TABLE appointments ADD CONSTRAINT no_overlap
  EXCLUDE USING gist (doctor_id WITH =, tstzrange(starts_at, ends_at) WITH &&)
  WHERE (status IN ('held', 'booked'));
```

**Booking flow**

1. **Search** reads a cached availability view (Redis or a read replica), updated on every booking. A slightly stale list is fine; the write path is the source of truth, and a lost race returns `409` with the nearest alternatives.
2. **Hold** with one atomic conditional update (#552, MySQL syntax). Expired holds count as free, so no cleanup job is needed for correctness:

   ```sql
   UPDATE slots
   SET status = 'held', held_by = :patient, hold_expires_at = NOW() + INTERVAL 10 MINUTE
   WHERE id = :slot
     AND (status = 'free' OR (status = 'held' AND hold_expires_at < NOW()));
   -- 1 row changed → you hold it; 0 → someone else does
   ```

3. **Pay or verify insurance** with an idempotency key (#356, #429).
4. **Confirm** only if the hold is still yours (`WHERE id = :slot AND held_by = :patient AND hold_expires_at > NOW()`), insert the appointment and an outbox row in the **same transaction** (#484). If payment succeeded after the hold expired, try to re-hold, or refund automatically.
5. **After commit**, the outbox relay publishes `AppointmentBooked`; consumers create the video room, schedule reminders, update the doctor's calendar and analytics (#554). Each consumer is idempotent.

**First available doctor** is a matching problem, not a calendar: keep a queue of waiting patients and pick a free doctor with `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`, so concurrent requests don't wait on the same row.

**Time zones:** store instants in UTC. Keep schedule rules in **local time with an IANA zone** (`Europe/Warsaw`) and expand them to UTC per day, because a 9:00 shift is a different UTC time in winter and summer. On the spring-forward night a 02:30 slot doesn't exist. Show times in the patient's zone.

**Reminders and no-shows:** schedule reminders as delayed jobs (a table polled with `SKIP LOCKED`, or delayed messages, #535) that check the appointment is still active before sending. Mark no-shows from video session events and apply the cancellation policy.

**Failure modes to mention:** payment succeeded but confirmation failed (a reconciliation job against the payment provider), a double submit (the idempotency key), a notification provider outage (retries and a dead-letter queue), a doctor removing hours that already have bookings (a domain rule: reject, or move the bookings and notify the patients), and replica lag showing a just-booked slot as free (the write path rejects it).

**Privacy:** the reason for a visit is health data: keep it out of logs, analytics and notification texts, and audit who reads it (#556).

**Follow-up:** a popular doctor opens next month's calendar at 8:00 and 500 patients try to book in the first minute. What happens, and what would you improve? (The conditional update keeps it correct; improve the experience with a virtual queue or a waitlist, return alternatives on `409`, and protect the endpoint with rate limits.)
