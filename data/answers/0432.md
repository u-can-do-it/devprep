## Hints

- When a deadline is truly fixed, which of scope, quality and time can still change?
- When and how did you communicate risk to stakeholders?
- What did you change afterwards so the same crunch didn't happen again?

## Answer

Interviewers want to see that you stay **effective and calm**, make **smart trade-offs**, and **communicate**, rather than just working until midnight.

**Structure with STAR:**
- **Situation and task:** the deadline and why it was fixed (a launch event, a regulatory date, a customer contract), and what was at risk.
- **Action:** show the useful behaviors:
  - **Clarified the must-haves**, negotiated scope ("we ship the core flow, and export goes into the next release"),
  - **broke the work down**, found the riskiest part and tackled it first,
  - **communicated early**: flagged risk to the PM or lead with options, not just bad news,
  - protected quality where it matters (tests on the critical path, a feature flag for a safe rollout) and wrote down any shortcuts as explicit tech debt,
  - coordinated with the team: split work, unblocked others, avoided duplicate effort.
- **Result:** delivered (or partially delivered, with honest reasons) and the impact.
- **Learning:** what you changed in planning, estimation or process afterwards.

**What interviewers look for:** prioritization, composure, transparency with stakeholders, ownership, and a sustainable attitude. Occasional crunch is reality, but it shouldn't be the default way of working.

**Red flags:**
- Heroics as the whole story ("I worked 80-hour weeks") with no prioritization or communication.
- Silently cutting quality (no tests, skipped reviews) and never mentioning the consequences.
- Blaming management, or claiming you "never feel pressure".
- No reflection on how to prevent the situation next time.
