## Hints

- What question do you ask before "how should I build it"?
- How do you make progress when nobody can answer your question this week?
- When is it right to push back on a feature, and how do you do it without being the person who always says no?

## Answer

Many offers ask for "product mindset" and "working with ambiguous requirements". Interviewers want to see that you **clarify the problem**, **make reasonable decisions with incomplete information**, and **communicate trade-offs**, not that you wait for a perfect spec or build exactly what the ticket says.

**A structure for your answer:**

1. **Understand the problem, not just the request.** Ask *why*: what user problem does this solve, how do we know, what does success look like (a metric), and who is affected? "Add an export to Excel" might really mean "managers need to see monthly totals", which a report page might solve better.
2. **Clarify scope and constraints.** Edge cases, permissions, volumes, deadlines, what's explicitly out of scope. Write the open questions down and get answers from the product owner, designer or users.
3. **Write down assumptions** where answers aren't available, and share them ("I'm assuming time-off requests can't span two years; tell me if that's wrong"). That makes progress possible and keeps decisions reversible.
4. **Offer options with trade-offs:** a quick version, a full version, and what each costs and risks. Recommend one.
5. **Reduce uncertainty cheaply:** a prototype, a spike, a feature flag rollout to a few users, or the smallest slice that delivers value and teaches you something.
6. **Confirm and record the decision** in the ticket or an ADR, so later "why did we build it this way?" has an answer.

**Pushing back constructively:**

- Base it on **users, data and cost**, not personal preference: "Support data shows people want X; this solves Y. Building it costs two Sprints and adds a new permission model."
- Propose an alternative instead of just saying no.
- Accept that product decides priority. If the decision goes the other way, **disagree and commit**: build it well and measure the result.

**Example to prepare (STAR):** a feature where clarifying the real need changed the solution, ideally with a result: less work, better adoption, or avoiding a costly mistake. Mention who you involved and how you communicated.

**Red flags to avoid:** "I just do what's in the ticket", building for weeks on unconfirmed assumptions, or blocking work until every question is answered.

**Follow-up:** the stakeholder who asked for the feature is unavailable for two weeks and the deadline is fixed. What do you do?
