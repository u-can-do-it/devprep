## Hints

- What problem do micro-frontends actually solve: code organization, or teams blocking each other's releases?
- How many teams need to ship the same frontend before a shared release train becomes the bottleneck?
- What can a monorepo with enforced module boundaries give you without composing apps at runtime?

## Answer

**Micro-frontends** split a frontend into pieces that are **developed, tested and deployed independently**, each owned by one team and usually cut along a business domain (search, product page, checkout, account). The pieces are composed into one product for the user. It is the microservices idea applied to the UI, and it's mostly an **organizational** pattern, not a technical one.

Two common ways to slice:

- **Vertical (by route or domain):** one team owns `/checkout/*` end to end, often including its backend. Simplest and most common.
- **Horizontal (several on one page):** a product page shows the gallery from one team, recommendations from another and the cart widget from a third. Needs runtime composition (Module Federation, Web Components, server fragments).

**Benefits:**

- **Independent deploys:** a team ships without waiting for a shared release train or for other teams' tests.
- **Team autonomy and clear ownership:** smaller codebases, faster CI, fewer merge conflicts.
- **Incremental migration (strangler fig):** new parts are built in a new stack while a legacy app (AngularJS, jQuery) keeps running, and are replaced route by route.
- **Fault isolation:** with error boundaries, one broken widget doesn't take down the whole page.

**Costs:**

- **Performance:** duplicated dependencies and extra network requests unless shared libraries are carefully configured.
- **Consistency:** the UX, design system and library versions drift between teams.
- **Cross-cutting concerns** become integration problems: auth, routing, analytics, i18n, error tracking, feature flags.
- **Harder local dev and testing:** you need to run or mock the shell and other parts, and e2e tests have to cover the composed app.
- **Version skew:** the host and parts are deployed separately, so contracts between them must stay compatible.
- **Ops overhead:** many pipelines, a manifest or routing layer, and a platform team to own the shell.

**When it's worth it:**

- Many teams (roughly 4–5+) working on one product, where the real bottleneck is release coordination.
- Clear domain boundaries that map to teams (Conway's law works for you here).
- Migrating a large legacy frontend gradually, or integrating apps that came from acquisitions or separate products.

**When it isn't:** one or two teams, a small app, unclear boundaries, or a problem that is really messy code rather than deployment coupling. Then a **modular monolith** is better: a monorepo (Nx, Turborepo, pnpm workspaces) with **enforced module boundaries** (Nx tags, `eslint-plugin-boundaries`), CODEOWNERS, fast affected-only CI and feature flags. That gives most of the ownership benefits with one build and one deploy. It can also be split later along those same boundaries.
