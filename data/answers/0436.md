## Hints

- What does this stakeholder actually need to decide or do with the information?
- Which everyday analogy maps onto the technical idea without being misleading?
- How do you check that they really understood?

## Answer

This tests **communication and empathy**, which are crucial as you grow, because engineers constantly explain trade-offs to product managers, designers, sales, support and executives.

**A practical approach:**
1. **Know the audience and the goal:** are they deciding something (budget, priority, timeline) or just need awareness? Start with what matters **to them**.
2. **Lead with the conclusion and its impact**, not the mechanism: "If we don't upgrade the database this quarter, we'll stop getting security fixes in March. It's two weeks of work and users won't notice anything."
3. **Use analogies carefully:** tech debt as interest on a loan, caching as keeping frequently used items on your desk instead of in the archive.
4. **Present options with trade-offs** in business terms: cost, risk, time, user impact. Give your recommendation.
5. **Skip jargon**, or define it once. Use visuals (a simple diagram, a before-and-after metric).
6. **Check understanding:** ask them to restate the decision or invite questions. Follow up in writing.

**In an interview, give a short real example (STAR):** a time you explained why a feature needed more time, why an outage happened, or why a refactor was worth it, and what the stakeholder decided as a result. You can also demonstrate live: "Let me explain what an API is to a non-technical person..."

**What interviewers look for:** clarity, adapting to the audience, focus on outcomes, and respect for non-technical colleagues.

**Red flags:**
- Condescension ("they just don't get it").
- Burying the listener in detail or jargon.
- Oversimplifying until the explanation is wrong, or hiding risks to avoid a hard conversation.
