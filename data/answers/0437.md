## Hints

- How do you judge impact and urgency, and are they the same thing?
- Who else should be involved when everything is "top priority"?
- How do you make sure the things you deprioritize don't silently fail?

## Answer

Interviewers want a **method**, plus evidence that you **communicate** instead of silently juggling.

**A practical framework:**
1. **Get everything visible:** list the tasks with their owners, deadlines and who is waiting on them.
2. **Assess impact and urgency.** Use an Eisenhower-style split: *urgent and important* (production down, a security issue, a blocker for others) comes first. *Important, not urgent* (a planned feature, tech debt) gets scheduled. *Urgent, not important* gets delegated or batched.
   - Ask: what's the **cost of delay**? How many users, how much revenue, how many teammates are affected? Is there a hard external deadline?
3. **Unblock others first:** a 15-minute review that unblocks two people often beats an hour on your own task.
4. **Align when priorities conflict:** if two stakeholders both claim top priority, don't decide alone. Bring the trade-off to your lead or PM: "I can do A by Thursday or B by Thursday, not both. Which matters more?"
5. **Communicate what's slipping:** tell the people affected early, with a new estimate.
6. **Protect focus:** time-box interruptions, batch small tasks, and use the team's channel for support rotations.

**Give a short real example (STAR):** e.g. a production bug arriving during a feature deadline, how you triaged it, who you informed, and the outcome.

**What interviewers look for:** structured thinking, business awareness, proactive communication, and the ability to say "not now" respectfully. At senior levels, also prioritizing for the team, not just yourself.

**Red flags:**
- "I just work longer hours" as the whole strategy.
- First-in-first-out, or doing whatever the loudest person asks.
- Deprioritizing silently and missing deadlines without warning.
- Never escalating, or escalating everything.
